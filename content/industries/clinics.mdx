A clinic's intake problem is not indifference. It is that the person who answers the phone is also
the person checking in a patient, taking a payment, and finding a file. When those things collide,
the phone loses.

## How intake fails here

- **The phone rings during a consultation.** The call is not missed because it was unimportant. It is
  missed because the front desk is two feet from a patient who is mid-sentence.
- **Lunch and doctor-change hours are dead zones.** Enquiries arriving between sessions land in a gap
  nobody owns, and by evening they are stale.
- **WhatsApp gets read, not answered.** Someone opens the message between tasks, intends to reply,
  and the blue ticks tell the patient they were seen and ignored.
- **Nothing distinguishes urgency.** "I need a check-up next month" and "chest tightness since this
  morning" arrive in the same queue, in the same tone, and get the same non-answer.
- **Returning patients are treated as strangers.** Someone who has been coming for four years
  re-explains their history to whoever picks up.

The compounding cost is not the single missed call. It is that a patient who could not reach you at
9:12 has already found a clinic that answered by 9:20, and will keep going there.

## What changes

The front desk stops being the first layer. It arrives to appointments that are already booked,
already triaged, and already attached to the right clinician, and spends its attention on the
patients physically in front of it.

Reminders go out before the appointment, with instructions on what to bring, which is the cheapest
available reduction in no-shows. Every decision the layer made is logged with the rule that produced
it, so the clinic can see what was treated as urgent and adjust the threshold if it was wrong.
