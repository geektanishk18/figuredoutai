The people qualified to answer technical questions are the people currently on a roof.

## How intake fails here

- **Technicians are on site, so nobody answers.** The office phone is covered by whoever is not out,
  which on a busy day is nobody.
- **Urgency is indistinguishable.** A bedroom unit not cooling and a server room climbing through
  thirty degrees arrive as the same call. One of them is an emergency.
- **AMC clients are not recognised.** A contracted customer waits in the same queue as a cold caller,
  which is the fastest way to lose a renewal.
- **Jobs are scoped on the call, badly.** "AC not working" dispatches a technician who arrives
  without the diagnostic tools, the ladder, or the part.
- **Repeat visits are absorbed as normal.** A second trip for a part that could have been identified
  in the first conversation is a day of margin gone.
- **Commercial enquiries are handled like residential.** A four-unit VRF fault routed to a
  split-system technician wastes both the visit and the relationship.

## What changes

Dispatch receives a scoped job instead of a callback request: system type, symptom, contract status,
units affected and an access window, with the priority already decided by rules you wrote.

The office phone stops being the bottleneck between a failing system and a technician. And because
every dispatch decision is logged with the rule behind it, you can see whether your priority
thresholds actually match how your engineers would have triaged the same call.
